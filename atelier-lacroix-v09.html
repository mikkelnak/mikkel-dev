On Detail,   Patience,   & The Weather

Detail Holds Weather.

Marginalia

A reveal of four millimetres is not decoration. It is the breath the building takes before snow.

Every junction in an alpine house is a small treaty with the sky. We draw each one at full scale, in pencil, on paper that thickens through a winter at the desk. A copper flashing turned a quarter-inch deeper will hold a generation of meltwater; a sill cut without shadow gap will, within a decade, begin to weep.

Fig. 01 — Larch-on-granite reveal, Maloja, 2024

The detail is therefore not the ornament of a building — it is its memory of climate. We keep a workshop of full-scale mock-ups beside the office in Sils, where a joint may sit for an entire season before it is permitted into a drawing.

The highest of our completed houses sits at two thousand eight hundred and forty-seven metres. Its rooms were tuned not in plan but in section — to the thinning of air, the lengthening of shadow, the particular blue of late afternoon at altitude.

— Folio III
On Rooms   &   The Thin Air Above Them

Rooms Tuned To Altitude.

Marginalia

Above two thousand metres, a ceiling of three metres feels low. We measure rooms in breath, not in feet.

A room at altitude is not the same room translated. Sound carries differently in thinner air; a fire draws harder; the body, slower in oxygen, asks for longer thresholds between warmth and the cold beyond the glass. We design accordingly — entry sequences of three doors, not one; window seats deep enough to nap in; hearths sized for the patience of a long afternoon.

Fig. 02 — South sitting room, Haus Stüva, 2,140 m

Light, too, behaves differently here. It arrives later, leaves earlier, and is — at midwinter — so low across the snow that a single south window can fill a room as if from below. We orient our houses to this fact, and to little else.

— Folio IV

Folio V  ·  The Studio

On Atelier Lacroix.

Atelier Lacroix was founded in Sils, in the autumn of 2014, by Hélène Lacroix — a Genevoise architect who, after a decade in the larger offices of Zürich and Basel, returned to the Engadin to build, slowly and at small scale, the houses she had drawn as a student. The practice has remained small by design: never more than seven people, never more than three buildings underway at once. We have refused, on principle, more commissions than we have accepted.

Our work is principally domestic — chalets, refuges, the occasional library or chapel — though we resist the word chalet when it implies a costume. The buildings we make are quiet, durable, and made from materials that age with their valley: larch, granite, lime plaster, oiled linen. We draw mostly in pencil. We model in card and balsa. We visit our sites in every season before a single line is laid.

The studio publishes a slim monograph each spring, of which this page is the ninth. It is, like the houses, an act of slow assembly — read, we hope, with the same patience it asked of its makers.

— H. L., Sils, May MMXXVI

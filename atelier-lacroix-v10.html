Chalets Cut from Light

Three houses, two valleys, and a quiet argument for slow architecture — assembled at the foot of a mountain that has not been in a hurry for ten thousand years.
From the Editor
— Pages 04 / 05

There is a particular hour, in late January, when the Engadin valley turns the colour of unbleached linen. The sun does not so much set as withdraw; it leaves the snow behind, lit from within, and any building careless enough to interrupt that light declares itself at once. The chalets gathered in this issue were drawn — slowly, and largely by hand — in deference to that hour.

We have spent the last winter walking sites with the studio: through pine litter at Sils, across the moraine above Verbier, along the threshold of a barn in Champéry whose original carpenter is still, at ninety-one, in correspondence with us. What follows is less a catalogue than a notebook. It is offered in the hope that buildings, like seasons, are best read at their own pace.

Marthe Lacroix-Renaud Editor · Sils-Maria · February MMXXVI

Detail Holds Weather

On the patience of larch facades, the geometry of a one-millimetre rebate, and what it means to design a building that is permitted to age in public.

Words by Henri Pellet  ·  Photographs by Camille Vuillemin

Hand-joined larch facades are scribed to the slope and left to silver over a decade1. Each lap, each shadow gap is dimensioned for the season it must survive — snow load computed against the angle of the prevailing wind, drip lines hidden inside a one-millimetre rebate. The chalet ages in public; the joinery does not.

The studio works from a small library of profiles, none of them new. A board, planed once, will move twice — by season and by sun — and a facade is essentially a long, slow conversation about that movement. The carpenters at the studio’s yard in Maloja can name the tree a piece came from, and frequently do2.

It is tempting, in a building magazine, to make a virtue of complication. The detail that holds weather is rarely complicated. It is, almost always, the one that has been drawn three times: first to be elegant, then to be honest, then to be repairable by a roofer in February with cold hands3.

We asked one of the studio’s associates how she knew a junction was finished. When you can no longer see where the drawing ends and the weather begins, she said, and turned the page.

A building learns its climate the way a coat learns a shoulder — slowly, and only by being worn.
End Notes
  1. Larch sourced from the studio’s standing contract with a forest cooperative above Pontresina; harvested only in waning-moon weeks, as the carpenters insist.
  2. See Carnet du Charpentier, the studio’s in-house log, kept continuously since 2014 and bound annually in linen.
  3. A principle attributed, in the studio, to the late Engadiner builder Andri Caduff (1934–2019), who would not allow a drawing to leave the table unless he could draw it again, from memory, in pencil.

Rooms Tuned
to Altitude

Plaster at 1,800 metres reads differently. A short essay on the geometry of December light, the framing of a single view, and the discipline of building windows last.

Words by Élise Dorier  ·  Photographs by Tomas Krieg

II.
Feature · Interior

Air at 1,800 metres reads differently against plaster. The studio works the geometry of each room to the angle of December sun, then builds the windows last — frameless, set deep, sized to a single view4. Stone underfoot stays cool through August and warm through February; the house, in either season, is simply still.

The decision to delay the windows is, in practice, the decision to design twice. The room is built; the room is then lived in for a week, in a tarp-walled mock-up, in weather. The mason’s chalk lines for the opening are corrected by hand, then corrected once more by the joiner. Only afterwards is glass ordered5.

What one is looking for, ultimately, is the moment a room and a view stop arguing — when the wall has been thinned enough, and the sill set deep enough, that the mountain seems to belong to the house and not the other way round.

End Notes
  1. All glazing detailed in collaboration with the Glarus workshop of Bürgi & Fils, whose patience the studio considers a structural element.
  2. A practice borrowed, the studio admits, from the late Sigurd Lewerentz, by way of an essay read aloud in the office one slow March afternoon.
Élise Dorier is the studio’s associate for interiors and a contributing editor.
